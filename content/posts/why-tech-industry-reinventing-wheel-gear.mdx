Every few years, engineers seem to rediscover inventions that have already been solved. From cloud platforms re‑creating Heroku‑style rollouts on Kubernetes to century‑old screw‑driven vehicles resurfacing in modern prototypes, the pattern is unmistakable. This relentless reinvention wastes time, inflates costs, and often delivers marginal gains. For gadget lovers, understanding why it happens can guide smarter purchasing decisions.

<Callout type="takeaway">Reinventing the wheel rarely adds value and usually costs more.</Callout>

## What happened
Heroku pioneered rolling deploys, blue/green releases, and health‑checked rollbacks a decade ago. Today, many teams rebuild that same machinery on Kubernetes—configuring readiness probes, surge tuning, and custom rollback logic—only to discover the platform gives them primitives, not the convenience they once enjoyed. The same pattern repeats with authentication, background jobs, retries, and rate limiting, where countless providers exist yet engineers still write ad‑hoc implementations that introduce subtle bugs.

Historical attempts to “reinvent” the wheel echo the same mindset. In 1899 Jacob Morath patented a tractor that used counter‑rotating augers instead of wheels, and later screw‑driven vehicles like the Armstead Snow Motor tried to replace treads. Though none achieved lasting commercial success, the ideas resurfaced in niche applications, showing how old concepts are repeatedly revived.

A recent breakthrough adds a literal twist: researchers published a shape‑shifting tire that transforms from a tall‑and‑skinny profile to a short‑and‑fat one using a waterbomb origami tessellation. The wheel can bear more than 2,248 pounds even during transition, and its diameter shifts from roughly 0.8 m to 0.46 m, promising adaptable performance across terrains.

## Why it matters
When companies rebuild solved systems, they consume engineering cycles that could innovate truly new features. The hidden cost appears as longer time‑to‑market, higher maintenance overhead, and increased risk of subtle failures. For consumers, this means paying premium prices for products that are merely repackaged old tech, or buying into hype without measurable benefit. The origami wheel illustrates that genuine innovation can emerge when a clear performance gap is addressed rather than when a familiar problem is re‑engineered for its own sake.

## How to think about it
Treat each “new wheel” claim as a hypothesis. Verify whether the product solves a problem you actually have, and ask for independent benchmarks rather than marketing hype. Prioritize solutions that build on proven foundations with clear, measurable improvements. When evaluating gear, compare the feature set against existing standards and calculate the true cost‑benefit before committing.

<Callout type="warning">Avoid buying products that claim to be a “new wheel” without clear performance data; they may be rebranded old tech.</Callout>
