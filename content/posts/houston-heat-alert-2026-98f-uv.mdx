Houston is bracing for a sweltering day with temperatures projected to reach 98 °F and a heat index climbing as high as 103. The forecast calls for mostly clear skies, calm winds, and an ultraviolet index that will stay in the extreme range for several days. At the same time, ozone concentrations near the ground are expected to rise, worsening air quality across Harris, Brazoria, and Galveston counties. Residents need to understand why these conditions matter for health, especially for those with heat‑related illnesses or respiratory sensitivities.

## What happened
The National Weather Service reported that early this morning Houston’s temperature hovered around 78 °F with a heat index of 81, but temperatures are set to surge into the high 90s by afternoon. The heat advisory notes a moderate‑to‑high risk of heat‑related illnesses across Harris County and surrounding areas. Additionally, an air‑quality alert highlights elevated ozone levels that could trigger respiratory issues for sensitive groups.

Meteorologists also warned that the ultraviolet index will remain in the “extreme” category, increasing the risk of sunburn and long‑term skin damage. The combination of high heat, strong UV, and poor air quality creates a perfect storm for health concerns, prompting local officials to advise residents to limit outdoor exposure and stay hydrated.

## Why it matters
Prolonged exposure to temperatures near 98 °F can lead to heat exhaustion, heat stroke, and dehydration, especially for the elderly, children, and those with chronic conditions. The elevated UV index amplifies the risk of severe sunburn, eye damage, and skin cancer over time. Poor air quality compounds respiratory problems, affecting asthma sufferers and anyone with lung sensitivities. Together, these factors strain emergency services and increase the likelihood of heat‑related hospital visits.

## How to think about it
Plan outdoor tasks for early morning or late evening when temperatures are lower and UV exposure is reduced. Keep water bottles handy and sip regularly; aim for at least one liter per hour of activity. Wear lightweight, light‑colored clothing with UPF protection, and apply broad‑spectrum sunscreen with SPF 30 or higher every two hours. Consider portable cooling accessories such as battery‑powered fans, cooling towels, or misting neck wraps to lower body temperature. If you have a health condition that makes you vulnerable, stay indoors in air‑conditioned spaces during peak heat hours.

<Callout type="warning">Do not engage in strenuous outdoor activity during peak heat (12 PM‑4 PM) without proper hydration, shade, and cooling gear; heatstroke can develop rapidly.</Callout>
